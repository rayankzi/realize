# The Philosophy and Origins of Computer Science

**Topic/Category:** Computer Science / AI / History of Computation
**Primary Goal of the Post:** To redefine Computer Science not as the act of building apps or websites, but as a unique discipline that sits at the intersection of mathematics, engineering, and empirical science.

---

## Executive Summary

This post presents a collection of "private note fragments" from GreyFox's notebook that trace the evolution of Computer Science from its roots in pure mathematical abstraction (Turing's "On Computable Numbers") to its modern embodiment in AI and robotics. It argues that CS is a "novel category of knowledge" because its abstractions (code) have the unique property of being self-executing, bridging the gap between theoretical logic and physical reality.

## Key Insights & Main Ideas

- **CS is not Software Engineering:** The post asserts that making apps and webpages are consequences of the discipline, not the discipline itself.
- **The "Triple Nature" of CS:** Computer Science is simultaneously:
    - **Mathematics:** When producing formal proofs about algorithms and computability.
    - **Engineering:** When building systems that must function under physical constraints.
    - **Empirical Science:** When training models (AI) on data and validating them against observable reality.
- **The "Execution Property":** Unlike topology or number theory, formal systems in CS (like a Turing machine) can be instantiated in hardware and "pointed at reality." The abstraction *becomes* the process.
- **Borrowing from Nature:** Modern AI represents a shift where CS moved from first principles to borrowing designs from neuroscience (neural networks), inheriting empirical science's methods (validation through testing rather than formal proof).
- **The Universal Substrate:** CS is described as a medium that can run any abstraction and project it into the physical world through hardware.

## Detailed Breakdown / Step-by-Step

- **Slide 1: Title & Abstract:** Introduces the "Architect's and Scientist's classification dilemma." Traces the crossing from Turing's machines to embodied AI.
- **Slide 2: The Birth in Pure Abstraction:** Focuses on Alan Turing (1936) and Alonzo Church. CS began as a mathematical study of what is computable, not as an observation of natural phenomena.
- **Slide 3: The Property That Changed Everything:** Explains the transition from theoretical construct (Turing machine) to physical realization (transistor/processor).
- **Slide 4: Borrowing a Brain:** Discusses the transition to AI. Mentions McCulloch and Pitts (1940s) and Rosenblatt's Perceptron (1958), marking the shift toward models based on biological neural architecture.
- **Slide 5: Getting a Body:** Argues that mounting a network in a robot makes the system empirical. It must be tested against the "resistance of the real world" (gravity, wet floors), moving it into the realm of science.
- **Slide 6: The Classification Debate:** A dialogue between the author (GreyFox) and an Electrical Engineering Professor. The professor characterizes CS as a "mental game" that requires making connections others can't.
- **Slide 7: Conclusion & References:** Summarizes the "Universal Substrate" and provides a bibliography including Turing, Church, Von Neumann, LeCun, and Norvig. Ends with a note that the human brain is the "best computer."
