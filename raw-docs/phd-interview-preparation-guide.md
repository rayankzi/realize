# PhD Interview Preparation Guide: Fully Funded @ Oxford

**Topic/Category:** Education / Academic Career
**Primary Goal of the Post:** To provide a comprehensive framework and list of 10 essential questions to help applicants prepare for a PhD interview, specifically tailored for economics and public policy but applicable across disciplines.

---

## Executive Summary

This post serves as a high-value guide for prospective doctoral students, breaking down the PhD interview into a structured preparation process. It emphasizes that an interview is not just about intelligence but about demonstrating a "researcher's mindset"—clear interests, critical thinking about methods, and departmental fit. The guide covers everything from the initial "Tell us about yourself" to complex questions about causality, data, and future career plans, ending with tactical advice for the interview panel.

## Key Insights & Main Ideas

- **Thinking Like a Researcher:** The panel evaluates four key levels: the policy problem, the economic mechanism, the evidence (methods), and the potential contribution.
- **Academic Self-Introduction:** The goal is to introduce yourself as a future researcher, not just a student, by linking your background to your research interests.
- **Feasibility is Crucial:** Questions about research gaps, causality, and data sources are designed to check if the proposed project is practical and academically meaningful.
- **Causality & Methodology:** For economics/policy, being able to discuss specific identification strategies (e.g., Diff-in-Diff, IV, RCTs) and their assumptions is vital.
- **Departmental Fit:** Applicants must show they are "academically informed" about the specific university, supervisor expertise, and how they will contribute to the research community.

## Verbatim Templates & Scripts

> **1. Tell us about yourself (Example structure):**
> "My academic background is in economics, with a particular interest in applied microeconomics and policy-relevant research. Through my dissertation and research experience, I became interested in how economic constraints shape individual, household, or firm decisions. I am now applying for a PhD because I want to develop the skills to produce independent research and contribute to evidence-based economic debates."

> **2. Why do you want to do a PhD? (Example structure):**
> "I want to do a PhD because I am interested in questions where the existing evidence is incomplete or contested. I am especially drawn to economics because rigorous theory and empirical methods can help us understand behaviour, incentives, and policy effects. A PhD would allow me to develop the methodological depth and independence needed to contribute original research."

> **3. What is your proposed research question? (Example structure):**
> "My proposed research examines how financial constraints affect education or labour market decisions among young people. More specifically, I am interested in whether access to credit, scholarships, or income support changes human capital investment and early career choices. The broader question is how liquidity constraints shape inequality and long-term economic mobility."

> **5. How would you identify causality? (Example structure):**
> "To identify causality, I would look for a source of variation that affects treatment exposure but is plausibly unrelated to other determinants of the outcome. If a policy was introduced at different times across regions, a difference-in-differences design may be appropriate. The key concern would be whether treated and control groups followed similar pre-treatment trends."

> **9. What can you contribute to the department? (Example structure):**
> "I hope to contribute intellectually and practically to the department. My background has given me experience in quantitative research, independent writing, and empirical analysis. I would be keen to participate in seminars, reading groups, and workshops, and to contribute to the PhD community by sharing skills in coding, methods, or research organisation."
